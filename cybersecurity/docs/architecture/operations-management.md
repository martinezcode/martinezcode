# Core Competencies in Security Operations Management

## Physical, Operations, & Personnel Security

<!-- Header (End) -->

<!-- Body (Start) -->

**Introduction**

Physical and information security operations present many organizational challenges. Organizations may choose to separate physical security from information security in the organizational structure, or they may combine them into a single department. In either case, executive management must possess skills in the core competencies that are fundamental to successful security operations. The EC-Council CCISO Domain Details provides an outline of these core concepts: access control, social engineering, phishing attacks, identity theft, physical security, risk management, disaster recovery, business continuity planning, firewall, intrusion detection systems (IDS), intrusion prevention systems (IPS), network defense systems, wireless security, virus, trojans, and malware threats, secure coding best practices, securing web applications, hardening operating systems, encryption technologies, vulnerability assessment, penetration testing, computer forensics, and incident response.

**Access Control**

Access control measures are necessary for most organizations to provide resources to individuals or groups who need them, and prevent access from outsiders or unauthorized insiders. Mandatory and discretionary access control criteria must be identified so that an access control plan can be implemented (EC-Council). Knowledge of identity verification systems is generally required, and procedures should be developed to ensure that users who are granted access have a full understanding and awareness of their responsibilities (EC-Council).

**Social Engineering, Phishing Attacks, Identity Theft**

Malicious actors employ social engineering and phishing attacks in attempts to gain access to protected resources. They may assume false identities for in-person attempts to manipulate members of the organization into providing physical access or over-the-phone or email attempts to gather information or entice users into installing malware. If information is compromised, insiders may have their identities stolen for use in gaining access to resources or committing other crimes. Managers must have a full understanding of these threats, and be prepared to design necessary security controls and response plans (EC-Council).

**Physical Security**

Physical security measures are critical for defense of organizational premises, protection of physical and on-site information assets, and ensuring the safety of personnel, customers, and visitors. Management must be able to determine asset values and potential impacts from loss of resources, and they must be able to establish measurement systems along with a holistic physical security plan that aligns with the overall organizational security goals (EC-Council).

**Risk Management**

Managers must be able to balance organizational security concerns with allowances for some level of acceptable risk. Coordination of risk management teams across the organization will be needed to develop structured risk assessment and management programs that are regularly updated as threats and vulnerabilities continuously emerge and evolve (EC-Council).

**Disaster Recovery and Business Continuity Planning**

Security managers should understand that perfect security is unattainable, and incidents will inevitably occur. If the worst-case security scenarios or natural disaster events take place, disruption of business operations may result. Preparation for these events with disaster recovery and business continuity plans may minimize disruption. Management must be able to identify and prioritize critical business functions, and have backup and recovery solutions in place along with standard operating procedures for implementation in the event of a disaster (EC-Council).

**Firewall, IDS/IPS, Network Defense Systems, Wireless Security, Virus, Trojans, and Malware Threats**

With technology playing ever increasing roles, information security threats may come from a variety of attack vectors. Managers in security are now expected to have a broad understanding of the firewalls, intrusion detection and prevention systems, and other network defense systems that are available to secure wired and wireless networks (EC-Council). Antivirus and malware prevention and removal tools should be utilized as well (EC-Council).

**Secure Coding Best Practices, Securing Web Applications, Hardening OS, Encryption Technologies**

Software tools and web applications are now widely used, and the communication and storage of private and proprietary data is common within these systems. Security management must be able to ensure that software and web systems are developed and operated securely, with configurations that minimize vulnerabilities and provide for robust testing, monitoring, and analysis (EC-Council). Operating systems (OS) that provide a platform for software and web applications must be hardened, so managers need an awareness of the system logging and configuration management tools that are available (EC-Council). Knowledge of encryption technologies used in secure communication and storage is also necessary to ensure the security and privacy of organizational data (EC-Council).

**Vulnerability Assessment and Penetration Testing**

Physical and information operations present distinct vulnerabilities, but the security of both can be vetted with penetration testing. Managers should be prepared to design, develop, and implement penetration testing programs that can identify vulnerabilities and provide reporting to enable the correction of issues (EC-Council).

**Computer Forensics and Incident Response**

When incidents do occur, security management must be able to respond appropriately. Investigations will often be necessary, so familiarity with computer forensic techniques is required (EC-Council). The forensic investigations should be incorporated into a robust incident response plan that can assess violations and their impact, while preserving evidence and engaging with legal departments and law enforcement if necessary (EC-Council).

**Non-Core Competencies and Driving Factors**

These operational competencies are considered core competencies because they "refer to the fundamental abilities a protective program needs in order for it to deliver services effectively" (McCrie, 2016). The overall needs of organizations vary, but the core competencies are universal to modern security programs. Executive management must be well-versed in these concepts, and they must be able to delegate tasks to senior security personnel who possess the core skills to initiate new programs, operate existing programs, and handle personnel administration (McCrie, 2016). The list of above core competencies is not all-inclusive, and will evolve over time as security needs change and new threats and vulnerabilities arise. Other non-core competencies may be required, depending on the needs of the organization. For example, expertise in Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) is not a core competency because HIPAA regulations apply only when protected health information (PHI) is involved. A manufacturing company may not need security managers with experience in HIPAA compliance, but a hospital or health insurance company surely would.

In addition to understanding the core competencies, security managers must also be aware of the factors that drive security operations. Each organization will have its own set of priorities and challenges, but its security operations are likely to be influenced by several common factors: cost savings, risk mitigation, income generation, crime, fear, litigation, insurance against liabilities or negligence, legal mandates, bureaucratic requirements, and accreditation requirements (McCrie, 2016).

**Conclusion**

Some combination of the core competencies and driving factors will determine the organization's security priorities, and the weight of each factor may vary across industries, sectors, and organization types. With a broad understanding of the core competencies and driving factors, security management professionals can be prepared to lead organizational security operations. Organizational needs are always unique, but the core competencies and driving factors are fundamental to physical and information security management.
