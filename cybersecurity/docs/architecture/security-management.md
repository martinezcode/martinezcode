# Classical & Modern Security Management

## How has organizational security evolved over the past 100 years?

<!-- Header (End) -->

<!-- Body (Start) -->

The development of security management theories has occurred relatively recently when compared to classical management theories. McCrie (2016) noted that security operations have existed throughout the modern era, but the consideration of security as a well-defined discipline in organizational management structures was not widely practiced until the mid 1900s. Indeed, many small and medium sized organizations still do not operate with security as stand-alone aspect of management. However, with the rise of information technology, global communication, and online commerce, security has become a major concern, and many organizations have introduced executive level positions and specialized departments dedicated to security. It is now common for organizations to incorporate both physical and information security management as foundational components, although placement varies within organizational structures.

Professional development of security programs occurred gradually, as discussed by McCrie (2016):

> Only in the last half of the twentieth century did security emerge as a defined, usual, respectable, and visible part of management. In the process, security operations have been enhanced by the writings and practices of those who have directed successful programs (p. 16).

The founding of professional associations like ASIS International (ASIS) in 1955, the Security Industry Association in 1967, and the National Council of Investigation and Security Services in 1975, and the International Security Management Association in 1976 are counted as milestones in the growth of the field (McCrie, 2016). These organizations contributed to research that helped develop standards and practices now widely used in business management.

Others, such as Ronald V. Clarke, promoted situational crime prevention and opportunity theory as methods of identifying and reducing risk using low cost methods. Clark proposed that decreases in crime could be achieved by interventions based on three primary factors: "a motivated offender, a suitable reward or goal for the offender's actions, and the absence of appropriate controls that could check such action by the offender" (McCrie, 2016). Researchers such as J. Kirk Barefoot, and Richard K. Paterson also made contributions by encouraging internal investigation and risk management techniques as ways to prevent loss and off-load risks to third parties such as insurance providers (McCrie, 2016).

Important progress has also been made with efforts to widen diversity in security management. Throughout much of the early 20th century, security employment populations were largely male. McCrie (2016) recognized inclusivity advances in Darlene Sherwood's 1985 appointment as the first female president of ASIS and the success of Bonnie S. Michelman as leader of ASIS while also directing "security operations at one of the nation's leading medical campuses," starting in 2001 (McCrie, 2016, p. 20). Even in the 21st century, lack of ethnic and cultural diversity in security professions has been a problem in the United States. In 2011, the government began efforts to foster inclusion when then President Barack Obama signed "Executive Order 13583, Establishing a Coordinated Government-Wide Initiative to Promote Diversity and Inclusion in the Federal Workforce, in order to promote the federal government as a model of equal opportunity, diversity, and inclusion," as described by the Department of Homeland Security ("Commitment to a Diverse Workforce," n.d.).

Now that security has been widely incorporated into mainstream management structures, it makes sense to apply classical management theories in the security context as well. Security personnel can be organized as theorized by Max Weber (1864 - 1920) and Chester Barnard (1886 - 1961), whose classical "bureaucracy" rationale is still commonly used to manage large-scale security operations (McCrie, 2016). Barnard was also a proponent of "acceptance theory," a method of encouraging compliance within an organization (McCrie, 2016).

Large-scale security operations may often involve repetitive tasks. Enterprise-level organizations can increase efficiency by taking advantage of the principles Frederick W. Taylor (1856 - 1915) developed for enhancing industrial productivity and applying them within the security context (McCrie, 2016). Another classical management tool that can benefit organizations of any size is the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats (SWOT) matrix popularized by the work of Albert Humphrey in the 1960s and 1970s (McCrie, 2016). These tools have traditionally been used for organizational planning, but can be applied to security programs in the modern environment.

Early in the 1900s, organizations would naturally have emphasized the protection of assets using physical security measures. Most businesses focused on production of goods and provision of services, with security concerns as an afterthought. But the development of vast communication networks over the 1900s and into the 21st century has brought a need to prioritize the defense of information assets. Intellectual property and customer data have become critical components of modern business, and threats against these assets are continually increasing. In recent times, mobile technology and remote work has introduced new security challenges, as Whitman and Mattord (2017) noted:

> In the last 30 years, however, technology has moved out from the data center to permeate every facet of the business environment. The business place is no longer static; it moves whenever employees travel from office to office, from city to city, or even from office to home (p. 3).

Many of the security management concepts that have emerged over the latter part of the last century have become critical components of successful security programs as businesses face these modern challenges. Organizations and professionals working in the security field should also leverage the classical management theories that have proven successful over time. By incorporating both schools of management theory, businesses can improve security without sacrificing profitability and security professionals can become better leaders.
