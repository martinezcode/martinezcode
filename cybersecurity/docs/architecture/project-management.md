# Turn an Idea into Reality with Project Management

<!-- Header (End) -->

<!-- Body (Start) -->

As a software developer, I view proper management as the key to project success. Software starts with an idea and ends with an application, and reaching that goal is a multiple step process. Breaking that process down into an organized project helps us achieve results in a way that can be extended to apply to nearly any information technology goal. By utilizing the concepts of project management, any organization can turn an idea into a reality.

In *ICT Project Management*, Films on Demand (2011) suggests that project steps can be modeled as a life cycle with multiple phases: conception, planning, building, testing, implementing, and evaluating. Examining these phases will demonstrate a method for project success.

Conception usually begins with an idea, a team, and a proposal (Films on Demand, 2011). By critically reviewing the idea as a team at this stage, the goal can be simplified down to the essential requirements, and informed decisions can be made about how to move forward.

With a clear goal, the team can begin planning. This stage may be the most critical toward the success or failure of the project. Mistakes made in this phase can carry forward, resulting in wasted efforts or inability to meet requirements in the later stages. Films on Demand recommends a thorough systems analysis to facilitate proper allocation of resources (2011). Other project management tools can also be incorporated to aid in the process. Whitman and Mattord (2017) highlight a work breakdown structure (WBS) as a simple planning tool that breaks down tasks into a list detailing the effort, skill, and dependencies that will be required. Large teams or those working with significantly complex projects may require more advanced tools. Gantt charts and network scheduling tools such as Program Evaluation and Review Technique (PERT) and Critical Path Method (CPM) diagrams can provide a visual reference to clarify project plans and tools such as Microsoft Project are widely used for automated project management (Whitman and Mattord, 2017).

When a detailed plan is in place, an organization is set up for success in the building and testing phases. Team members should be able to follow their assignments to complete tasks efficiently. They may begin with a preliminary design, such as a mock up or prototype, which enables a visual review and a chance to work out the details before a more exhaustive effort is put into building a functional product (Films on Demand, 2011). As the product takes shape, a thorough testing phase should work through any issues that arise.

The final phases of implementation and evaluation will likely be an ongoing process. As end users begin working with the product, training will be necessary, and ongoing maintenance will take place as the product is evaluated (Films on Demand, 2011). Issues appearing during these phases may be a sign of success, as they indicate that people are using the product.

At any point during this process, conflicts may arise. Differences in opinion are a necessary and expected aspect of project management. When team members are invested in a project, we should expect them to think critically and bring their own ideas forward, even when they oppose another viewpoint. By having a clear plan in place, with established requirements and common goals, the team can work through issues with a constructive approach that should ultimately benefit the project. The project life cycle and project management tools provide a clear path through such pitfalls on the journey from idea to reality. By following these concepts of from the beginning, an organization will increase their chances of success.
